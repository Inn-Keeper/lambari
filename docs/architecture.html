One transaction, end to end

The tutorial version: follow a single bet-sized payment through the pipe. Steps are the real order of operations — each one maps to code you can open.

  1. Produced. The load generator marshals a transaction and produces it to transactions, keyed by card hash — so this card's events all land in one partition, in order.
  2. Fetched. The consumer polls a batch. Anything that fails to decode is published to transactions.dlq with origin topic/partition/offset headers; it never blocks the batch and never vanishes.
  3. Scored. SubmitBatch fans the batch across the worker pool and blocks until every transaction has run the five-rule chain — amount, card velocity, IP fan-out, geo mismatch, high-risk merchant. Points sum; ≥40 opens a review, ≥70 declines.
  4. Made durable, then committed. Flagged verdicts are published (keyed by TxID) and a case opens. The verdict and DLQ producers are flushed, and only then are the offsets committed. Kill the process anywhere in this window and the batch replays — duplicates possible, no record lost. make e2e proves exactly that with a SIGKILL against a real broker: it asserts every verdict reaches the topic. It says nothing about the case queue, which is in-memory and per-process and does not survive the restart at all. Where duplicates land is stated once, in the README's Delivery semantics; it is deliberately not restated here.
  5. Streamed. Once per second the API pushes an SSE frame — stats, recent verdicts, case counts — and the dashboard's reducer applies it. The frame counter doubles as the review queue's refetch signal.
  6. Triaged. The queue lists open cases worst-first and freezes while your pointer is over it. Resolving is optimistic: the row leaves immediately, comes back with an error if the server refused, stays gone with a note if the case was already evicted.
  7. Labeled. The resolution — confirmed fraud or false positive — is stored. That label stream is the feedback loop the whole design points at.

Decisions you have to defend

These are independent, not sequential — expect them in any order, and expect pushback on whichever one you sound least sure about.

Scale split

"Millions of requests per minute" — how much of that actually needs a synchronous decision?

Why it matters
If 5% needs a verdict, the hard problem is ~1.6k decisions/sec under an SLO plus cheap bulk ingest. That's a different system than 33k decisions/sec.
Move
Ask before sizing anything. Reframing their number beats being impressed by it.
Data boundary

The platform provider is B2B. Which signals are actually yours?

You see
Bets, rounds, outcomes, table state, device and timing telemetry.
The operator sees
Deposits, withdrawals, KYC, payment instruments, cross-operator identity.
Move
Name the boundary before proposing rules. Most candidates will design the operator's system by mistake.
State on rebalance

Velocity windows are in-process. Partition assignment is dynamic. What happens on a scale event?

The failure
A player's window silently resets on whichever pod receives the partition. No error, just worse detection.
Options
Shared Redis · Flink keyed state with checkpoints · static assignment and accept it.
Move
Flink — it's on the target stack, and the windowing is the hard part anyway.
Partition ceiling

Why doesn't HPA-on-CPU work for a Kafka consumer?

The trap
A consumer group can't usefully exceed its partition count. Scale to 80 pods against 48 partitions and 32 idle pods still bill you for their requests.
Generalizes
Kinesis bills per shard, per hour: 33k/sec needs 34+ shards used or not. Your parallelism ceiling and your bill are the same number.
Move
KEDA on consumer lag, maxReplicas pinned to partition count.
Delivery semantics

Commit before or after the engine finishes scoring?

The bug
Committing after enqueue is at-most-once. A crash drops everything still buffered — in a fraud system, silently. And async producers add a second window: a verdict still in the client buffer dies with the process even though its offset was committed.
Move
At-least-once: commit only after scoring, flush the producers before every commit. Where the resulting duplicates land is a longer answer with three parts, and it lives in one place — the README's Delivery semantics — rather than being paraphrased here. Same shape as a retried wallet call, which is a connection worth making out loud.
Hot key skew

You keyed by playerId for ordering. What does a whale do to that?

The failure
One player generating 1000× the events pins one partition, and one consumer, while the rest idle. Adding partitions doesn't help.
Move
Name the tension honestly: the key that gives you ordering is the key that gives you skew.
Latency budget

How long do you actually have?

The constraint
Live casino decides inside a betting window. Late betting — exploiting stream delay to bet on a knowable outcome — is the signature attack, and it's a pure timing problem.
Move
Ask for the budget in milliseconds, then design backwards from it.
False positive cost

Where do you set the threshold?

The asymmetry
Blocking a legitimate VIP costs far more than in e-commerce — high-value players are a large share of revenue. The costs are nowhere near symmetric.
Move
Lead with this. Reasoning about thresholds without it sounds like payments fraud experience applied blind.

Narrating it

Six beats, in order, every time you're asked something open-ended:

  1. Restate and bound. "Scoring bets inline, latency budget matters, we can't lose events. I'll assume under 100ms — correct me."
  2. Name the axis before the options. "The tension is consistency against throughput." This is the beat that reads as senior.
  3. Two or three options. Three is the ceiling. A survey reads as hedging.
  4. Pick one, out loud. "I'd do B."
  5. Name the tripwire. "I'd revisit if whale traffic skews the partitions."
  6. Say what you don't know, fast and without apologizing. "I don't know how MGA treats automated declines for audit — I'd check before designing the appeal path."

Beat six is the one everyone hides and the one that separates senior from mid. Bluffing to a senior reviewer is the only unrecoverable move.

Glossary

Every term on the board. Definitions are written for why the thing matters here, not for a textbook — if a term has a trap attached, the trap is the definition.

Event streaming
Topic
A named stream of records. game.events in, verdicts out.
Partition
The unit of both parallelism and ordering inside a topic. Records are ordered within a partition, and not ordered across them.
Key
The field that decides which partition a record lands in. Same key, same partition, so ordering is guaranteed per key — which is why velocity rules need playerId as the key.
Consumer group
A set of consumers splitting a topic's partitions, where each partition goes to exactly one member at a time. That "exactly one" is where the scaling ceiling comes from.
Offset
Your position in a partition. Committing an offset tells the broker you're done up to that point — so when you commit is what determines your delivery guarantee.
Consumer lag
How far behind the head of the partition you are, in records. The single best health metric for a streaming system, and the right thing to autoscale on.
Rebalance
Reassignment of partitions when group membership changes. Every deploy, crash, and scale event causes one — which is why anything held in process memory is at risk.
OnPartitionsRevoked
The callback fired just before a partition is taken from you. Your last chance to finish in-flight work and commit. Not having one is a silent data-loss window.
DLQ — dead letter queue
A side topic for records you can't process, so a poison record neither blocks the stream nor disappears without trace.
At-most-once
Commit before processing. Crash and the record is gone. Almost never what you want, and easy to build by accident.
At-least-once
Commit after processing. Crash and you reprocess — so you may see a record twice. The usual correct choice, provided the work is idempotent.
Exactly-once
Kafka can give you this within a read-process-write transaction. It doesn't extend to side effects outside Kafka, which is why people over-trust it.
Idempotent
Doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once. What makes at-least-once safe, and the same property a retried wallet call needs.
Hot key / skew
One key producing far more traffic than the rest, pinning one partition and one consumer while others idle. Adding partitions doesn't help. A whale does exactly this.
Backpressure
Letting slowness travel upstream instead of buffering without limit. Here: a bounded channel, so polling slows and lag becomes visible rather than the process running out of memory.
Flink
A stream processor that owns your per-key state for you and restores it after failure or reassignment. On the target stack, and the direct fix for state dying on rebalance.
Keyed state
State the framework holds per key and moves with the key when partitions are reassigned. The thing in-process maps can't do.
Checkpoint
A periodic consistent snapshot of that state, replayed on recovery. What makes keyed state survive a crash.
Changelog topic
Kafka Streams' version of the same idea — state changes written to a compacted topic so another instance can rebuild from it.
Scaling & cost
Pod
The unit Kubernetes schedules and scales. Here, one consumer process.
Replica
How many copies of a pod are running. For a consumer group, more than the partition count buys nothing.
HPA — Horizontal Pod Autoscaler
Kubernetes' built-in scaler, on CPU by default. The wrong signal for a Kafka consumer: a backed-up consumer can be lagging badly at low CPU.
KEDA
Event-driven autoscaling that reads external metrics like consumer lag, and can scale to zero. The right scaler here.
maxReplicas
The scaler's upper bound. Pin it to the partition count, or you pay for pods that are structurally incapable of doing work.
Requests
CPU and memory the scheduler reserves for a pod, used or not. Requests decide how many nodes you rent, so requests are the bill.
Limits
The hard ceiling. Exceed the CPU limit and you're throttled; exceed the memory limit and the kernel kills the container.
Cluster Autoscaler
Adds and removes nodes when pods can't be scheduled. Where HPA stops and the actual cloud bill starts.
Bin packing
Fitting pods onto as few nodes as possible. Over-stated requests wreck it — every pod reserves room it never uses.
Kinesis shard
AWS's partition equivalent: 1 MB/sec or 1,000 records/sec, billed per shard-hour whether you use it or not. 33k/sec needs 34+ shards. Makes the ceiling-equals-bill point unusually literal.
MSK
Managed Kafka on AWS. Same shape, billed per broker instead of per shard.
The engine
Worker pool
A fixed set of goroutines pulling from one queue, rather than a goroutine per request. Bounds concurrency instead of letting load decide it.
Bounded channel
The queue in front of those workers, with a fixed capacity (16,384 here). Full means the producer waits — that's backpressure working.
Sharded state
Sliding-window counters split across 256 mutex shards keyed by FNV hash, so workers don't contend on one lock.
FNV
A fast non-cryptographic hash. Used only to pick a shard, so speed matters and collision resistance doesn't.
Atomics
Lock-free counters. Let the stats endpoint read without ever stalling the scoring path.
Sliding window
"How many events for this key in the last N seconds." The core primitive under every velocity rule, and the state that rebalancing destroys.
Additive scoring
Each rule returns points plus a named flag; the points sum to a verdict. Tunable in one file, and explainable by construction.
Velocity rule
Rate of events per key over a window. Catches bursts a per-event rule can't see.
IP fan-out
One IP touching many distinct accounts. The multi-accounting signal, and the enabler under most collusion.
Jurisdiction check
Is this player playing from somewhere the licence covers? A compliance requirement first and a fraud signal second.
p50 / p99
Median and 99th-percentile latency. p99 is the one that matters — it's the experience of the unluckiest 1%, and averages hide it completely.
Latency budget
The total time available before the decision is worthless. In live casino it's set by the betting window, not by preference.
SLO
The target you commit to and alert on, e.g. "p99 scoring under 50µs". Distinct from what you happen to measure once.
False positive
Blocking a legitimate player. Costs are wildly asymmetric here — one blocked VIP can outweigh many missed small frauds.
Live casino domain
Operator
The consumer-facing casino brand. Owns the player relationship: deposits, withdrawals, KYC, self-exclusion.
Provider / supplier
The company building the games and platform the operator serves. The side this system sits on — and the side whose data it actually has.
Live casino
Real dealers, real tables, streamed to players in real time. Physical equipment and video delay both become attack surface.
Betting window
The seconds in which a bet is accepted for a round. Your latency budget, handed to you by the game.
Late betting
Exploiting stream delay to place a bet once the outcome is already knowable. The signature live-casino attack, and purely a timing problem.
Multi-accounting
One person running several accounts. Rarely the end goal — it's the setup for collusion, chip dumping, or promo abuse.
Collusion
Players coordinating across seats at a table, or occupying every seat to control the game.
Chip dumping
Deliberately losing to a confederate to move value between accounts. A laundering pattern, not a gambling one.
Bot play
Scripted clients playing optimal strategy. Shows up in timing distributions — telemetry that is genuinely yours.
Whale / VIP
A very high-value player. Drives the false-positive asymmetry, and drives partition skew.
KYC / AML
Know Your Customer and Anti-Money-Laundering — identity and source-of-funds obligations. The operator's, mostly, which is exactly why the data boundary matters.
MGA · UKGC · Spelinspektionen
Regulators for Malta, the UK, and Sweden. They set what you must be able to prove after the fact, which is why explainability isn't optional.
Verdict
The output: approve, review, or decline. Review is the interesting one — it's a human queue, not a block.
Case
A flagged verdict an analyst works, worst score first.
Label
The analyst's resolution — confirmed fraud or false positive. This is the loop that makes a model possible later, and why the queue is worth building before the model.
